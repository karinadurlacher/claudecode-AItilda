# Patient factors associated with treatment response, loss of response and clinical outcomes in ADHD: limited evidence synthesis

## 1. Question
Which baseline clinical, biological, or other patient factors are associated with treatment response, loss of response to treatment, and clinical outcomes in patients with ADHD? (Source: `research-question.md`, status CONFIRMED 2026-11-02. Human patients with ADHD, children, adolescents and adults; pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatment; publications 2022-01-01 to 2026-11-02; English. "Loss of response" is operationalised as non-response over time, tolerance, treatment failure, or discontinuation for lack of efficacy.)

How to read citations: each study is cited as author, year and PMID, matching the entry in section 8 (References) and the row in `outputs/evidence-table.md`. Where an author has two 2026 papers, the PMID distinguishes them.

## 2. Summary

Overall certainty: **very low**. Most studies are abstract-only, small, exploratory, or use persistence/discontinuation as a proxy for loss of response; five carry a high-concern appraisal. No factor has consistent, replicated support in this set. The "certainty" labels below are this draft's informal reading of the appraisal flags; no formal GRADE assessment was done.

1. **Treatment persistence, switching and discontinuation (proxy for loss of response).** In large register and clinical cohorts, neuropsychiatric comorbidity, older age at initiation and female sex were associated with earlier discontinuation or shorter time on treatment, and ASD comorbidity with more switching in children. Sources: Brikell 2026 (PMID 42392681), Garcia-Argibay 2026 (PMID 42331561), Garcia-Argibay 2026 (PMID 42580811), Wettstein 2026 (PMID 42878250, abstract only). Certainty: **low** (large samples, but moderate provisional concern, proxy outcomes, tables not read for two of the four, one abstract-only). The associations differ in direction by age group for conduct/oppositional defiant disorder (see section 5).
2. **Clinical response in clinic samples.** Older age was associated with response, and borderline intellectual functioning, mild intellectual disability, conduct disorder, ASD and adjustment disorder with lower odds of response (Gul 2026, PMID 42528343). Alcohol and cannabis use disorders were associated with lower odds of response in 67 adults, with very wide confidence intervals (Carbone 2026, PMID 41976997, high concern). Certainty: **very low**.
3. **Brain imaging and genetic markers.** Several baseline imaging markers (white-matter connectivity deviation, cortical gyrification, sulcal depth, network connectivity in youth with familial bipolar risk, striatal DAT binding) are reported as associated with treatment outcome, nearly all from abstracts without effect sizes, and none replicated across independent studies in this set. A candidate-gene finding (SNAP25 rs362998) comes from 42 children (Todurga-Seven 2026, PMID 42940731, high concern), while a multi-cohort polygenic score analysis in 1000 cases found no significant association (Rovaris 2026, PMID 41655471). Certainty: **very low**.
4. **Prediction models and long-term outcomes.** Machine-learning models of adult persistence and functioning performed weakly (AUCs .52-.58, best AUC .58; van der Plas 2026, PMID 42873248, abstract only). An acupuncture-plus-conventional-care model reported AUROC 0.83 internally and 0.78 to 0.87 externally but names no predictors (Li Y 2026, PMID 42258584, abstract only). Models in an MPH versus neurofeedback RCT had "limited predictive value" (Bohner 2026, PMID 42517414, abstract only). Certainty: **very low**.
5. **Null results.** No predictor of improvement was significant in 48 Thai children (Punyapas 2026, PMID 42845572, high concern); no polygenic score was associated with methylphenidate response (PMID 41655471); and the trial-level analysis describes the evidence on clinico-demographic predictors as limited and inconsistent (Roy 2026, PMID 42595937, abstract only).

### 4.2 Findings by factor

#### 4.2.1 Brain imaging and neurobiological markers

*Evidence.*
- Baseline deviation of white-matter structural connectivity from a normative age trajectory predicted 12-week response to atomoxetine but not methylphenidate; 2-year decreases in deviation tracked symptom improvement (Xu 2026, PMID 42675180; abstract only; no numeric effects).
- Positive region-specific associations between frontal cortical gyrification and treatment response across three symptom dimensions (Laatsch 2025, PMID 41107253; abstract only; no numbers). Sulcal depth in parietal, temporal and occipital regions predicted symptom alleviation, and genetic susceptibility (polygenic scores) influenced the relationship (Laatsch 2026, PMID 40983149; abstract only; no numbers). Both are COMPAS secondary analyses with overlapping samples; an erratum exists for PMID 40983149 (10.1016/j.bpsc.2025.12.006) whose content could not be retrieved.
- Familial bipolar I risk (high-risk, HR, vs low-risk, LR): HR-MAS showed a greater increase in right AMY-VLPFC negative functional connectivity than LR-MAS (P = 0.034) and HR-PLA (P = 0.007); LR-MAS had greater reductions in ADHD total and inattention symptoms than HR-MAS, with no numbers given (Li Q 2026, PMID 42863796; abstract only). In the RCT, baseline network dysconnectivity predicted treatment outcome in the HR-MAS group only (Qin 2025, PMID 40712681; partial full text; no numbers). These two studies overlap and are not pooled.
- Higher baseline putamen DAT binding predicted improvement in sustained attention; changes in DAT/NET binding did not correlate with cognitive improvement (Oya 2025, PMID 41128391; 21 adults at baseline, 12 re-evaluated; high concern; outcome is a cognitive test, not a symptom scale; no numbers).
- In 36 adults, frontal eye field (FEF) and intraparietal sulcus (IPS) activation increased with methylphenidate in the medication-naive group but not the non-naive group; the correlation between FEF activation and symptom reduction in the naive group was moderate and did not survive multiple-comparison correction (Shimizu 2026, PMID 42790052; abstract only; clinical response is a secondary measure).

*Interpretation.* Imaging markers are repeatedly reported as associated with outcome, but each rests on a single study, mostly on abstracts without effect sizes, with small samples in the PET, fMRI and bipolar-risk studies. The set does not show that any marker is usable to guide treatment. The atomoxetine-specific finding (PMID 42675180) is not tested against other studies here.

*Uncertainty.* 5 of the 7 imaging-related studies are abstract-only (PMIDs 42675180, 41107253, 40983149, 42863796, 42790052); PMID 41128391 is high concern; the COMPAS and NERF pairs share samples; PMID 40983149 has an unread erratum.

#### 4.2.2 Genetic factors

*Evidence.*
- In 42 children and adolescents aged 6-16 treated with methylphenidate for two months, the rs362998 CT genotype was 34.78% in responders vs 5.26% in non-responders (0.026; FDR-adjusted 0.049) and the T allele was associated (0.036; FDR-adjusted 0.049); no haplotype association was significant after FDR correction (Todurga-Seven 2026, PMID 42940731; the p label is missing in the source text). Authors call the findings exploratory, with a small sample, wide confidence intervals and no replication.
- In a fixed-effects meta-analysis of 1000 ADHD cases from three cohorts, no polygenic score (ADHD, autism, bipolar, educational attainment, MDD, neuroticism, schizophrenia) was significantly associated with methylphenidate response; effect sizes were small with minimal heterogeneity (Rovaris 2026, PMID 41655471; no numbers; age group not reported in abstract).
- Polygenic scores for ADHD and cross-disorder susceptibility were examined as moderators of the sulcal depth relationship (PMID 40983149; see 4.2.1).

*Interpretation.* The two genetic studies test different things (a candidate variant in a small cohort vs polygenic scores in a larger multi-cohort sample), so they do not directly contradict one another. The larger null result and the high-concern appraisal of the small study both argue against treating the SNAP25 finding as established.

#### 4.2.3 Early physiological response to the first drug dose (not a baseline factor)

*Evidence.* In 171 children and adolescents, greater blood pressure increase at the first methylphenidate dose was associated with greater symptom reduction at 6 months: Inattention beta = 0.202, p = 0.015; Hyperactivity/Impulsivity beta = 0.225, p = 0.007; Combined beta = 0.233, p = 0.005 (D'Aiello 2025, PMID 40879766; abstract only; heart-rate result not reported in the abstract).

*Interpretation.* **The factor here is an early drug response, not a pre-treatment characteristic.** It may be informative after the first dose but does not answer the question of which baseline patient factors are associated with response.

#### 4.2.4 Clinical, comorbidity, demographic and treatment-history factors and treatment response

*Evidence.*
- In 1,314 children and adolescents (1,590 data points, because 276 patients were counted twice), older age was associated with response; severe disorder was associated with lower odds than moderate/marked severity; borderline intellectual functioning, mild intellectual disability, conduct disorder, ASD and adjustment disorder were associated with lower odds; ATX monotherapy had a lower likelihood of response than MPH+ATX (Gul 2026, PMID 42528343; odds ratios not in the abstract; tables not read; moderate concern; CGI-I outcome only; subtypes and IQ missing).
- In 67 adults (48 responders, 71.6%): alcohol use disorder OR = 0.079 (95% CI 0.010-0.595); cannabis use disorder OR = 0.243 (95% CI 0.068-0.860); ASD OR = 0.227 (95% CI 0.036-1.428) in the ASD model (Carbone 2026, PMID 41976997; high concern; retrospective, small sample, no systematic treatment exposure data, subjective CGI-I; p label missing in source text). The record's abstract describes ASD as a trend that did not retain statistical significance after adjustment.
- In 36 medication-naive adults followed for a mean of 116 days, executive function impairment and better quality of life were the best indicators of stimulant response; higher ASR Thought, Withdrawn, Internalizing Problems and Intrusive Thoughts scores indicated lower efficacy; poorer working memory and task monitoring predicted lower response (DiSalvo 2026, PMID 41948089; high concern; 18 per responder/non-responder group; no numbers in the abstract; direction of "executive function impairment" versus "poorer working memory" not clarified).
- In 246 adults on stimulants, higher personality dysfunction was associated with less improvement in functioning (beta = 0.44, p < 0.001) and more persistent ADHD symptoms (beta = 0.20, p < 0.001) (Jacobsson 2026, PMID 41906356; moderate concern; substantial attrition and irregular follow-up).
- In 48 newly diagnosed Thai children followed for 24 months, symptoms, functional impairment (d=-0.79) and school difficulties (d=-1.02) decreased by 24 months, but there were no significant associations of improvement with any tested predictor (age, sex, caregiver/parental status, subtype, comorbidities, parenting score), all p>0.05 (Punyapas 2026, PMID 42845572; high concern for the predictor question; small n may limit power; pandemic-related missing data at 18 months).
- In 146 children in an RCT of methylphenidate versus home neurofeedback, both prediction models of emotional/executive variables had "limited predictive value"; parent-rated internalizing problems fell with d = -0.409 (95% CI -0.603, -0.215) for MPH and d = -0.396 (95% CI -0.574, -0.217) for NF, with no between-intervention effect (Bohner 2026, PMID 42517414; abstract only; per-protocol sample).
- In 809 children treated with acupuncture plus conventional care, a machine-learning model using routine baseline data reached AUROC 0.83 (internal test) and 0.78 to 0.87 (external cohorts) for response, and external AUROCs 0.73 to 0.85 for adverse events; the predictors are not named in the abstract (Li Y 2026, PMID 42258584; abstract only).
- Across 62 RCTs, factors were examined in: age 56.4%, comorbidities 53.2%, sex 46.8%, baseline severity 27.4%, presentation 24.2% of RCTs; preliminary associations were found in: age 17.7%, comorbidities 17.7%, baseline severity 16.1%, sex 11.3%, presentation 4.8%; RoB 2 was high in 37% of trials (Roy 2026, PMID 42595937; abstract only). These are shares of trials, not effect sizes; no direction is given.
- Among 36 adults with ADHD and borderline personality disorder (24 completed follow-up), baseline anger dysregulation (P = 0.009) and mood stabilizer use (P = 0.029) were associated with continued MPH treatment (Brancati 2026, PMID 42127382; abstract only; the primary outcome is BPD features, not ADHD symptoms; causal conclusions cannot be drawn per the authors).
- Medication-naive versus non-naive status is reported under 4.2.1 (PMID 42790052).

*Interpretation.* Comorbid neurodevelopmental, intellectual-functioning and substance-use conditions point in the same direction (lower response) across PMIDs 42528343 and 41976997, but the two studies differ in age group, outcome handling and size, the adult study is high concern with very wide confidence intervals, and neither has been replicated here. The null result in PMID 42845572 and the "limited" and "inconsistent" conclusions in PMIDs 42517414 and 42595937 mean that the clinico-demographic evidence base is weak. Where models report good discrimination (PMID 42258584), the abstract names no predictors, so no patient factor can be identified from it.

#### 4.2.5 Persistence, switching and discontinuation (proxies for loss of response)

*Evidence.*
- In 1 000 411 new ADHD medication users from eight countries/regions, earlier discontinuation (>=180 day dispensing gap) in children was predicted by intellectual disability, autism and psychotropic medication use (HR range 1.32-1.51), while CD/ODD was protective (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.73 to 0.94). In young adults CD/ODD was a risk (HR 1.42, 95% CI 1.30 to 1.55). In adults, schizophrenia (HR 1.25, 95% CI 1.09 to 1.44) and tic disorders (HR 1.27, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.46) were associated with earlier discontinuation. In adolescents none remained significant after multiple-testing control (Brikell 2026, PMID 42392681; moderate concern, provisional; heterogeneity substantial and driven by US estimates; dispensing gap is a proxy).
- In 8961 children and adolescents in Norway, those aged 15-17 had 31.9 fewer days on treatment in year 1 (95% CI -40.8 to -23.1; p<0.001) than those aged 5-11, and females had -13.2 days (95% CI -19.8 to -6.5; p<0.001) (Garcia-Argibay 2026, PMID 42331561; moderate concern, provisional; prescription fills are not ingestion).
- In Swedish children and adolescents, ADHD with comorbid ASD (24,117) versus ADHD alone (79,830): treatment started more than 12 months after diagnosis in children 12% vs 7.7% and adolescents 14% vs 7.2%; mean switches in children 2.6 vs 2.1 (p<0.001); switch rate ratio in children 1.12 (95% CI 1.07 to 1.18); discontinuation within 3 months in children 16% vs 12% (Garcia-Argibay 2026, PMID 42580811; full text; moderate concern). The authors report no efficacy outcome and no reasons for switching. Within ADHD+ASD, no drug-versus-methylphenidate association survived Benjamini-Hochberg correction; the nominal atomoxetine and anxiety result in adolescents (IRR 0.83, 95% CI 0.73 to 0.95) had a corrected p=0.10. Drug-versus-drug comparison is a treatment contrast, not a patient factor, and is not evidence for this question.
- In 12,995 adults, time to transition off the initial stimulant (switch, non-stimulant, or discontinuation) was associated with hyperactive/impulsive presentation (HR 1.14, p=0.025; reference category not stated in the abstract) and objective impulsivity (HR 1.05, p=0.002), with an interaction with objective hyperactivity (p=0.003; HR 1.09) (Wettstein 2026, PMID 42878250; abstract only). For context only, stimulant class (a treatment variable): median 33.1 vs 15.0 weeks (dAMP vs MPH), MPH HR 1.45 (95% CI 1.36-1.55). Affiliations include a private company; conflict-of-interest statement not in the abstract.
- Anger dysregulation and mood stabilizer use were associated with continued MPH in ADHD-BPD (PMID 42127382; see 4.2.4).

*Interpretation.* Discontinuation or switching is not the same as lack of efficacy, and none of these studies separates lack of effect from side effects or other reasons. Within that limit, comorbid neurodevelopmental or psychiatric conditions in children and older age at initiation are associated with shorter time on treatment in more than one register study. The register studies may share source data (not verified), so the agreement should not be counted as independent replication.

#### 4.2.6 Long-term outcomes, remission and functioning

*Evidence.*
- In 133 adults with childhood-onset ADHD, childhood predictors gave persistence AUCs of .52-.58 (best AUC .58) and functioning best ridge R-squared (R2) = .02, RMSE = 2.07 (RMSE range 2.07-2.42) (van der Plas 2026, PMID 42873248; abstract only; low model performance; no retained predictors named).
- In 89 children with ADHD, remission at year 4 was 32.1% and persistence 67.9%; greater early reduction in teacher-rated inattention and lower baseline inattention independently predicted remission; the composite index AUC = 0.94 was labelled apparent, with external validation required (Durak 2026, PMID 42640016; abstract only; treatment context not stated). **The "early reduction" is not a baseline factor**; only lower baseline inattention is.
- In 351 adults in a methylphenidate trial and 6-month open-label phase (open-label N = 147), younger age predicted greater functional impairment, and the strongest predictor of functional improvement was greater symptom improvement (Weiss 2026, PMID 42084377; abstract only; no numbers; open-label phase uncontrolled; borderline for the inclusion criterion; authors have industry affiliations per PubMed). Symptom improvement is a concurrent response measure, not a baseline factor.
- Personality dysfunction and functioning: see 4.2.4 (PMID 41906356).

*Interpretation.* Childhood-to-adulthood prediction was weak in the one study that tested it directly with cross-validated machine learning (PMID 42873248), whereas the high AUC in PMID 42640016 is described as apparent and unvalidated. No long-term functional outcome in the set is tied to a validated baseline predictor.

### 4.3 Quality and risk of bias overview
- **High concern (5):** 42940731 (n=42, exploratory), 41948089 (n=36), 41128391 (21 adults, 12 re-scanned), 41976997 (n=67, very wide CIs), 42845572 (small n, missing data, null predictors).
- **Moderate (7):** 42580811, 42392681 (provisional), 42331561 (provisional), 41655471, 40712681 (provisional), 42528343, 41906356.

## 5. Conflicting evidence and uncertainty

**Evidence of disagreement or internal tension (shown side by side, not resolved).**
1. **CD/ODD and discontinuation by age (PMID 42392681, moderate provisional).** In children CD/ODD was protective (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.73 to 0.94); in young adults it was a risk (HR 1.42, 95% CI 1.30 to 1.55). Heterogeneity was substantial and driven by US estimates. No appraisal basis exists to decide which age-specific estimate to trust; both are reported.
2. **Conduct disorder in two outcomes.** In PMID 42528343 conduct disorder was associated with lower odds of response in children and adolescents, while in children PMID 42392681 reports CD/ODD as protective against discontinuation. The outcomes differ (CGI-I response vs dispensing gap), so these are not direct contradictions.
3. **Age in different directions across outcomes.** Older age was associated with response (PMID 42528343) but, at 15-17 years, with 31.9 fewer days on treatment in year 1 (PMID 42331561); younger age predicted greater functional impairment in adults (PMID 42084377). Populations and outcomes differ, so these results are not combined or ranked.
4. **Internal tension in PMID 41948089 (high concern).** Executive function impairment is listed among the best indicators of stimulant response, whereas poorer working memory and task monitoring predicted lower response. The abstract does not clarify the direction of each variable; no numbers are available to resolve this.
5. **Abstract versus text in PMID 41976997 (high concern).** The abstract summarises the alcohol use disorder OR as about 0.08-0.10; the full text gives OR = 0.079 (95% CI 0.010-0.595) in one model and a value of 0.074 in another model (table note); the value 0.074 lies just outside the abstract's range. The abstract reports the ASD result as a trend; the text OR = 0.227 has a CI of 0.036-1.428. All are reported as printed.
6. **Candidate gene versus polygenic score.** A SNAP25 association with methylphenidate response (PMID 42940731, high concern) and a null polygenic-score result (PMID 41655471, moderate concern) concern different factors and sample sizes and do not resolve each other.
7. **Marker versus change.** Baseline putamen DAT binding predicted sustained-attention improvement, but changes in DAT/NET binding did not correlate with improvement (PMID 41128391). Similarly, the FEF-symptom correlation in PMID 42790052 did not survive correction.
8. **Overlapping sample pairs.** PMIDs 41107253/40983149 (COMPAS) and 42863796/40712681 (NERF) report different analyses of the same samples; agreement between members of a pair is not independent confirmation.

**Null and weak results.** No PGS association with methylphenidate response (PMID 41655471); no predictor of improvement (PMID 42845572); "limited predictive value" (PMID 42517414); near-chance persistence AUCs and R-squared (R2) = .02 for functioning (PMID 42873248); no factor shown as a consistent predictor or moderator (PMID 42595937, authors' description).

**Uncertainty.**
- 14 studies are abstract-only, many without effect sizes; tables were not read in 11 others.
- Small samples: n=36 (PMIDs 41948089, 42790052, 42127382), n=42, n=48, 21 adults at baseline with 12 re-scanned.
- Observational designs: confounding by indication is a plausible risk for the persistence studies; one table note flags PMID 42580811's Table 1, which prints mean switches with an IQR that does not contain the mean (as in the source).
- 42528343 counted 276 patients twice.
- PMID 42878250 does not state the reference category for the presentation HR.

## 6. Limitations of the evidence and of this review
- **Search coverage.** PubMed only. Runs 1-7 retrieved only the first 10 (Runs 1-2) or 30 (Runs 5-7) hits per query, so for Run 1, 120 hits; Run 5, 70; Run 6, 305; Run 7, 101 (and the unquantified remainder of Run 2) were never retrieved; the related-articles list in Run 3 was not examined. Some title-screened but unrecorded hits were flagged in the log as possibly borderline (for example PMIDs 41873451, 41256939, 42807763, 42503306, 42481172, 42506346, 42415397, 42831355, 42892735, 42207988); they are pointers for follow-up, not evidence. Relevant studies are very likely missing.
- **Query design.** The queries emphasise medication; non-pharmacological interventions are scarcely represented in the results. Run 2's term "tolerance" returned noise.
- **Abstract-only basis.** 14 studies were extracted from abstracts; age range, country, effect sizes, limitations and funding are often "not reported in abstract". Appraisal cannot be done for these.
- **Partial full text.** 11 studies were read only in targeted passages; numbers come from abstracts unless marked "text" in the table.
- **Single-database, single-language.** PubMed only; English only, not filterable in the tool and checked per record.
- **Single reviewer.** The logs name one reviewer; no independent duplicate screening or extraction is recorded. QC was not completed.
- **Proxy outcomes.** Persistence, switching, discontinuation and dispensing gaps stand in for loss of response; reasons are rarely available. No study in the table defines loss of response after an initial response (for example tolerance) as its outcome.
- **Heterogeneous outcomes and factors.** Outcomes (CGI-I, SNAP-IV, CPRS, WFIRS, cognitive tests, remission, dispensing gaps) differ, so findings were not combined.
- **Borderline inclusions.** Four included studies are logged as borderline for the inclusion criteria (PMIDs 42863796 and 42790052, imaging is primary; 42127382, primary outcome is BPD features; 42084377, main predictor is a concurrent response measure). Two further studies have a factor that is not a baseline characteristic (PMID 40879766, early drug response; PMID 42640016, early reduction in inattention).
- **Date handling.** For PMID 42863796 the PubMed date is 2026-10-13 and the Crossref issue date 2027-01; the year is recorded as 2026 per PubMed.
- **Excluded and out-of-scope records** are not discussed as evidence.

## 7. Evidence gaps and suggested next steps

*Gaps (the table cannot answer these).*
- No study defines loss of response or tolerance after an initial response as an outcome; only discontinuation-type proxies are available.
- No baseline patient factor has replicated, consistent support, and no validated, clinically usable predictor is reported.
- Non-pharmacological treatment moderators are minimal in the table (acupuncture adjunct, PMID 42258584; neurofeedback, PMID 42517414).
- Adverse-event-driven discontinuation is not separated from lack of efficacy in the persistence studies; adverse events are modelled only in PMID 42258584 (no named predictors).
- Adolescents are poorly covered; PMID 42392681 found no significant indicators after multiple-testing control in adolescents.
- Effect sizes are missing for most imaging, genetic and clinical-scale findings.

*Suggested next steps.*
1. Obtain full texts for the 14 abstract-only studies and read the results tables of the 11 partially read ones; complete appraisal.
2. Retrieve and read the erratum for PMID 40983149 (10.1016/j.bpsc.2025.12.006).
3. Verify whether the Swedish, Norwegian and multinational registers overlap (PMIDs 42580811, 42331561, 42392681).
4. Resolve the three unresolved records (section 9) by user ruling.
5. Continue the search beyond the first 10-30 hits per query, add databases, and date-screen the Run 3 related-article list.
6. Re-run QC2-QC4 before any export or use.
